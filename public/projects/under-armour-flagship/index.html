Under Armour's first intelligent flagship

The store opened in Guangzhou as a testing ground for the future of Under Armour's retail. I was the analyst inside it: I ran its intelligent-store platform, designed how it measures the consumer journey, and wrote the KPI framework that moved it from static reporting to continuous measurement.

The store

Under Armour opened the store at Julongwan Taikoo Li in Guangzhou on January 23, 2026: the brand's first smart sports community space, which it calls a Sports House. Founder and CEO Kevin Plank flew in for the launch and ran a hybrid training race with local athletes alongside the APAC and China leadership. The space sits on an open platform run by an intelligent system that informs shoppers and helps staff run the floor, so the people on it can spend their time on expert service and relationships rather than on finding things out. There is an open stage for weekly sessions with athletes and coaches, large screens that carry local events, and product you can only get here, including a Guangzhou-exclusive early launch of a running shoe. CxC, the retail-innovation consultancy behind it, built it as a testing ground for the future of retail, and Under Armour says what is learned here will shape how it connects with athletes worldwide. The people in the first photo rarely stand in one room: global, regional and local leads came in for the opening because, as I saw it from inside, the store marked a second act for the brand, its founder back as CEO and technology becoming part of what Under Armour is.

Operator first, then teacher

I started as the sole operator of the store's proprietary intelligent-store platform, handling the software and running the analytics. By the end of the project, Under Armour's retail, creative, marketing and operations teams were running it themselves, because I trained them and wrote the handover documentation.

How the store sees a visit

The store watches itself the way a good floor manager does, minus the memory for faces. Cameras count people across gate lines and follow them as dots between zones; a dot that stops near a fixture is dwelling; a reach for a product moves its RFID tag and the camera sees the hand, and that is an engagement, which can call the product up on the LED wall beside it. Step through one visit; the real thing is in the clips underneath.

Measuring the consumer journey

The experience was designed around time: five moments from attraction to belonging, eleven touch points, and one measure the design team wanted the whole journey to raise, dwell, because time spent in a store leads to conversion, basket size and loyalty. Measuring dwell honestly was the first job of the model. I designed the model that measures the shopper journey from the store's cameras, RFID product tags and checkout (POS) data. It covers more than 100 tracked display areas and 190 products (SKUs), so merchandising could see which products and zones were performing instead of estimating.

A KPI framework leadership could run on

Every floor change becomes a test

When merchandising reset the floor by moving the store's modular fixtures and SKUs, I re-mapped the store's full digital layout (in retail, the floorset) in under 24 hours with minimal downtime. That turned each floor change into a repeatable test cycle: move something, measure it, keep what works. The map is everything: every count the store makes depends on knowing which product sits in which box, in which zone, under which camera.

The order of the re-map, as the configuration notes from February had it:

  1. The new floorset. Which products go on which fixture, from merchandising's plan, with the SKUs.
  2. The boxes. Redraw the product boxes fixture by fixture: one box per product group where colour variants sit together, smaller boxes where shoes stand apart, three to a section as the rule of thumb.
  3. The cameras. Point each fixture at the one camera that sees it best, so a product in two views is never counted twice.
  4. Gates and zones. Where people enter, where each zone starts and ends.
  5. The triggers. The pop-up each product calls, on which screen; tested by hand, one by one.
  6. Live. The dashboard open on the floor: counts flowing, heat on the right fixtures, the new map holding.

Sizing the rollout

I built a scenario model in Excel that let Under Armour size a chain-wide rollout across store tiers, projecting how per-store cost and cost per square foot fall with volume against break-even benchmarks. It started as the three-tier cost model in my weekly goals of August 2025: a first formula on the 15th, filled with project data a week later, a second version with an intro page by the 27th, variable costs such as software licensing and maintenance folded in during September. A model built on that thinking, from scratch and with illustrative numbers, is playable on its own page, with the full workbook to download.

The weekly rhythm

The hub only worked because it was written in every week. Monday set the goals, mid-week raised the blockers, Friday closed the week, and the Friday meeting with Under Armour's teams took the whole picture upwards. Every entry had to answer the same things: top three wins, challenges, support needed, next steps, and whether the week was ahead, on target or behind.

The handover kit

Operator first, then teacher: the last thing I built was the way out. In April 2026 I wrote the workspace guide for the two people taking over, 12 sections that explain what each room is for, how to keep it honest, and how to find out in a quarter of an hour what still needs doing. The training work sat in the tracker as 13 pieces: a handbook, a demo video, and documentation for the software, the signage, the audio, the analytics, store management, onboarding and the store network.

  1. Milestones, by status. Anything on hold or in progress.
  2. The tracker, filtered. In progress, testing or deployed; high priority; due in the next two weeks.
  3. The last two meeting pages. Summary first, then decisions and action items.
  4. The document hub. Any item marked done without its handover doc.

That is the guide's "new joiner scan", and its definition of done sits underneath it: a milestone counts as done only when the thing is deployed and the documentation that explains how to run it is linked.

What I'd do differently

We wrote the lessons down while they were fresh. These are mine, at the level that travels to the next store.

  • Tell every department early. A store like this touches retail, creative, marketing, IT, operations and the landlord. The context I was still explaining in month eight should have been planted in month one.
  • Build the lab before the store. Hardware and integrations went in during the build and the move-in, under time pressure. A small test lab first, which we set up in Shanghai in April, would have caught most of it.
  • Phase it, don't date it. Integrated experiences have dependencies: some things can't be judged until others are deployed. Phase one, phase two, with buffer, beats a launch-day checklist.
  • Content for the format, not from the archive. A wall like this needs content made for it, across sight and sound. Recycled campaign visuals read as noise. Decide stillness or motion early and commit to it.
  • Count the events apart from the days. A community event and a Tuesday afternoon are different traffic; one combined number hides both. A pioneering format needs its own measurement, which is what the KPI framework was for.
  • Give the flagship something nobody else has. The exclusive product was what people came for. The product plan has to match the space and the technology.
  • Programme the community daily. One event is a day; a club that meets there every week is a store that's alive.
  • Keep the space calm. In a digital environment the physical design should balance the sensory load, not add to it.
