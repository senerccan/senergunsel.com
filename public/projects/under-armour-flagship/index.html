Under Armour's first intelligent flagship

The store opened in Guangzhou as a testing ground for the future of Under Armour's retail. I was the analyst inside it: I ran its intelligent-store platform, designed how it measures the consumer journey, and wrote the KPI framework that moved it from static reporting to continuous measurement.

The store

Under Armour opened the store at Julongwan Taikoo Li in Guangzhou on January 23, 2026, with CEO Kevin Plank there for the launch. It was billed as the brand's first intelligent sports space: an interactive store whose smart system informs shoppers and helps staff run the floor. CxC, the retail-innovation consultancy behind it, built it as a testing ground for future retail.

Operator first, then teacher

I started as the sole operator of the store's proprietary intelligent-store platform, handling the software and running the analytics. By the end of the project, Under Armour's retail, creative, marketing and operations teams were running it themselves, because I trained them and wrote the handover documentation.

Measuring the consumer journey

I designed the model that measures the shopper journey from the store's cameras, RFID product tags and checkout (POS) data. It covers more than 100 tracked display areas and 190 products (SKUs), so merchandising could see which products and zones were performing instead of estimating.

A KPI framework leadership could run on

I wrote an eight-dimension KPI framework that maps what the store senses to the commercial metrics the business already runs on. It was presented to UA leadership as the roadmap from static reporting to continuous measurement.

Every floor change becomes a test

When merchandising reset the floor by moving the store's modular fixtures and SKUs, I re-mapped the store's full digital layout (in retail, the floorset) in under 24 hours with minimal downtime. That turned each floor change into a repeatable test cycle: move something, measure it, keep what works.

Sizing the rollout

I built a scenario model in Excel that let Under Armour size a chain-wide rollout across store tiers, projecting how per-store cost and cost per square foot fall with volume against break-even benchmarks. It started as the three-tier cost model in my weekly goals of August 2025: a first formula on the 15th, filled with project data a week later, a second version with an intro page by the 27th, variable costs such as software licensing and maintenance folded in during September. A model built on that thinking, from scratch and with illustrative numbers, is playable on its own page, with the full workbook to download.

Back of house: how the project ran

Every store has rooms the customer never sees that keep the floor running. On this project that was a Notion hub I built in July 2025, kept every week, and handed over in April 2026 with a guide for the people taking it on. I created and ran the weekly meeting with Under Armour's global and APAC leadership from it: progress, store performance and risks flagged early. Here it is, drawn as a plan. The counts come from the hub's own databases; nothing about how the store performed is in them.

What an intelligent store is made of

After the opening the tracker held 116 tasks across 11 systems, each with an owner, a priority, a date and the very next step. The bars are tasks per system; the solid part is what was complete or deployed by mid-March 2026.

The platform's one privacy rule, logged as a system of its own: no face recognition, no biometrics, no demographic profiling, and camera frames purged as soon as they are counted. The heatmaps are anonymised before they are stored. The same spirit as the door sensor on this site.

Counts and dates are from the hub's databases as exported in October 2026. Names, vendors, contracts, budgets and everything the store measured stay out.

The weekly rhythm

The hub only worked because it was written in every week. Monday set the goals, mid-week raised the blockers, Friday closed the week, and the Friday meeting with Under Armour's teams took the whole picture upwards. Every entry had to answer the same things: top three wins, challenges, support needed, next steps, and whether the week was ahead, on target or behind.

The handover kit

Operator first, then teacher: the last thing I built was the way out. In April 2026 I wrote the workspace guide for the two people taking over, 12 sections that explain what each room is for, how to keep it honest, and how to find out in a quarter of an hour what still needs doing. The training work sat in the tracker as 13 pieces: a handbook, a demo video, and documentation for the software, the signage, the audio, the analytics, store management, onboarding and the store network.

  1. Milestones, by status. Anything on hold or in progress.
  2. The tracker, filtered. In progress, testing or deployed; high priority; due in the next two weeks.
  3. The last two meeting pages. Summary first, then decisions and action items.
  4. The document hub. Any item marked done without its handover doc.

That is the guide's "new joiner scan", and its definition of done sits underneath it: a milestone counts as done only when the thing is deployed and the documentation that explains how to run it is linked.
