How to read it

Each street shows the share of the people walking past who would have to come in for the store to earn back its build in three years. It's a full store P&L: what's left of sales after product costs and sales costs like card fees, minus rent, staff, energy, licences and the screens' upkeep, with the first two years still ramping up.

The shaded band is what that kind of street can give. People on a destination shopping street stop more often than commuters on their way to a train, so the band is wider there. On the busiest streets it can also be cut short by the store itself: a store can only serve so many people.

Busy streets cost more rent, so the busiest street isn't automatically the best. But traffic grows faster than rent, which is why a small share of a big crowd is usually the easier sale.

How the two halves complete each other

Each half works on its own. Together they answer the questions neither can alone: which stores to open, where, and when the chain pays for itself.

The mix is the first thing the streets correct. I had started the model with an illustrative 30% flagships, 50% standard stores and 20% small ones. The streets that pay back say 24, 49 and 27: fewer flagships, more small stores. Then the chain itself changes the map: once its stores cost less, five more streets can pay one back.

Where it comes from

At Under Armour I built a scenario model in Excel to size a chain-wide rollout across store tiers: how cost per store and cost per square foot fall as you open more, against break-even benchmarks. The G07 model is built on that thinking, from scratch, with illustrative numbers. None of Under Armour's numbers are here.

The streets come from public data: two years of hourly counts from the City of Melbourne's pedestrian sensors, 1.6 million rows cleaned in Python, and the 78 most complete sensors sorted by how people move past them.
