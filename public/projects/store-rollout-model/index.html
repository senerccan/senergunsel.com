      What makes the next store cheaper

      Every line of a store's build sits in one of three groups, and each group gets cheaper in its own way as the chain grows.

      • Learning. Mostly labour: site preparation, walls and joinery, cabling, fitting rooms, systems integration, local design work, hiring and training. Each time the number of stores of a size doubles, these lines cost 10% less, down to 70% of the first store. A crew that fitted out a flagship has learned something for a small store too, so stores of the other sizes count for half.
      • Price breaks. Things you buy: flooring, lighting, fixtures and the screens. The discounts start at the 3rd, 8th, 15th and 30th store: 3%, 7%, 10% and 14%. Commit to the whole plan up front and every store gets the discount for the full order from day one.
      • No scale effect. Air conditioning and fire protection, storefronts, permits and launch events cost the same every time.

      The Cautious and Aggressive outlooks move those numbers both ways. Running costs scale too: the content platform licence gets cheaper per store as the chain grows, and one field technician looks after eight stores.

      Where the streets come in

      The model and my Tableau dashboard use the same 78 streets, the same rule for sizing them and the same store economics. The model hands the dashboard each size's first-store build, rounded to the dashboard's $50,000 steps: $1.4M for a flagship, $800,000 for a standard store and $450,000 for a small one. At those numbers 59 of the 78 streets can repay a store in three years.

      The dashboard hands back the mix those streets add up to: 24% flagship, 49% standard and 27% small, against the illustrative 30/50/20 I started with. That mix sets the sizes in the rollout: 7 flagships, 15 standard stores and 8 small ones out of 30.

      Then the loop closes. Once the chain is built, its stores cost 13 to 15% less on average than the first of each size, and at those costs 5 more streets pay back. A full store P&L, with rent that rises with traffic, staff, energy and screens to keep fresh, is stricter: 32 pass. Same store, two streets puts the two halves side by side.

      Where it comes from

      At Under Armour I built a scenario model in Excel to size a chain-wide rollout across store tiers: how cost per store and cost per square foot fall as you open more, against break-even benchmarks. This model is built on that thinking, from scratch, with new line items, new store sizes and new numbers. Every cost in it is illustrative, and none of Under Armour's numbers are here.

      The version on this page runs the workbook's rules in JavaScript and gives the same numbers, so you can play without downloading anything.
